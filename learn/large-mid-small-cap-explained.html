Large cap, mid cap and small cap explained

By Ali Arbab, founder of StockSaathi · Updated 7 November 2026

In India, large cap means the 100 biggest listed companies by full market value, mid cap means the next 150, and small cap means everything from the 251st down. SEBI set these definitions for mutual funds, and AMFI publishes the ranked list. Market cap measures a company's size, not its quality.

Key takeaways

  • SEBI defines large cap as the 1st to 100th company by full market value, mid cap as the next 150 and small cap as the 251st onward.
  • In AMFI's list for the six months to June 2026, the 100th company averaged about ₹1,06,346 crore and the 250th about ₹33,664 crore.
  • On StockSaathi's list, the top 100 companies hold 59.42% of total market value.
  • The typical top-100 share moved about 1.75% a day over the past year, against 2.24% for the typical share ranked 251st to 500th.

What is market capitalisation?

Market capitalisation, or market cap, is the total value the market puts on a company: its share price multiplied by its number of shares. A company with 10 crore shares at ₹500 has a market cap of ₹5,000 crore. A low share price doesn't make a company small, and a high one doesn't make it big, because the number of shares matters as much as the price.

Who decides the groups?

SEBI set the definitions in its circular of 6 October 2017, so that mutual fund categories would mean the same thing everywhere. Ranks are based on full market capitalisation. AMFI, the mutual fund industry body, publishes the ranked list every six months, using average market value across the exchanges. Funds then have one month to rebalance their portfolios if the list changes.

In the list for the six months to 30 June 2026, GAIL (India) was 100th at about ₹1,06,346 crore, and Bosch was 101st, only a sliver smaller. Godrej Industries was 250th at about ₹33,664 crore. So the line between two categories can be thin, and a company can cross it from one list to the next without much changing.

Why do price swings differ by size?

Smaller companies tend to move more. We measured how much each share's price typically moved in a day over the past year, and the median rises steadily as companies get smaller: 1.75% for the top 100, 1.94% for the next 150 and 2.24% for those ranked 251st to 500th. Below that, shares are smaller still and often trade much less, so a single order can move the price and it can be hard to sell when you want to. There are good reasons for the pattern.

A large company usually has several lines of business, a longer record and many more investors following it, so no single piece of news changes the picture as much. A small company may depend on one product or one customer. None of that makes one group better. A larger swing means more risk in both directions, and a share in any of these groups can fall a long way, as the record of stocks below their peaks shows.

How unequal is the market?

Very. On StockSaathi's list of 4,310 stocks with a market value, the top 100 hold 59.42% of the total, the next 150 hold 19.75%, and the thousands of companies below them share the remaining 20.84%. That is one reason an index like the Nifty 50 can tell you a lot about the biggest companies and very little about the rest.

What should a beginner take from this?

Size tells you about risk and how easily a share trades. It says nothing about whether a share is a good buy. Funds use these labels to describe what they hold, so they are worth knowing when you read about index and active funds.
