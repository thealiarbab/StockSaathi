Running a classroom stock market activity

By Ali Arbab, founder of StockSaathi · Updated 7 November 2026

A classroom stock market activity works best when students are marked on their reasoning, not their returns. Each student gets virtual money, writes down why they made each trade, and reflects every week. Avoid prizes and avoid ranking anyone by returns: both reward luck and risk-taking, which is the opposite of what you want to teach.

Key takeaways

  • Mark students on their written reasoning, not on how much their portfolio rose.
  • Avoid prizes: SEBI has cautioned about securities market competitions that offer prize money.

How do you set up a stock market activity in class?

Keep it simple. Check that your students are 13 or older, which StockSaathi's terms require, and follow your school's rules on student accounts and devices. Each student creates a free account, and they get ₹1,00,000 of virtual money.

There is an optional class code field when they join, so they can enter something like a class name, though StockSaathi has no teacher dashboard, and students share their work with you directly. Before anyone trades, agree the ground rules: this is virtual money, nobody will be compared by returns, and everyone's reasoning counts.

Nobody needs a demat account, a bank account or any payment details, and students under 18 can't deal with a real broker anyway. Do a five-minute check at the start of each class to keep the activity going, since most of the value comes from revisiting decisions and not from the first burst of excitement. A simple shared rule helps too: every trade needs one written sentence. That one rule turns a game into an exercise in thinking, and it gives you something to mark that has nothing to do with luck.

Why shouldn't you rank students by returns?

Because returns over a few weeks are mostly luck, and a ranking rewards the student who took the biggest risk. In a short contest the winning move is to put everything in one volatile share and hope, which is exactly what you don't want to teach. The student who diversifies and reasons carefully may finish in the middle, and a leaderboard tells them they were wrong.

It gives the wrong idea about markets too. A student who wins by luck learns that luck is skill, and one who loses by luck learns that thinking doesn't pay. Over a few weeks, the size of a gain says very little about the quality of a decision. A class that marks reasoning instead keeps everyone involved, including those whose picks fell, and it matches how good investors judge themselves. StockSaathi has no leaderboard for this reason, and nobody is ranked by returns.

Why avoid prizes?

Two reasons. Prizes push students towards gambling on one share. There is also a caution from the regulator: in a press release of 30 August 2016, SEBI noted that entities were soliciting investors through leagues, schemes and competitions related to securities markets, some involving prize monies, and said they were neither approved nor endorsed by SEBI or recognised exchanges. A classroom exercise with no money at stake is a very different thing, but keeping prizes out avoids the question altogether.

How do you judge learning instead of returns?

Mark what students wrote. Could they explain the business? Did they state a reason before each trade?

Did they notice a mistake and say what they would change? The report card on StockSaathi helps here, because it grades the quality of decisions rather than the size of gains, and students can show you theirs.

How do you handle losses calmly?

Plan for them. Some students will fall behind in week two, and that is the best teaching moment you will get. Ask what they expected and what actually happened.

Reading the panic-selling guide together works well. A crash replay is good for this too, because everyone goes through the same fall at the same time.

Frequently asked questions

How do you run a stock market game in class?

Give each student virtual money, require a written reason for every trade, set one reflection task a week, and mark the reasoning rather than the returns.

Should a school stock market game have prizes?

It is better without them. Prizes reward risky bets, and SEBI has cautioned about securities market competitions offering prize money.
