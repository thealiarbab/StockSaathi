How to invest as a student in India

By Ali Arbab, founder of StockSaathi · Updated 7 November 2026

Students aged 18 or over can invest like any adult once they complete KYC. Under 18 you can't buy shares through a broker, though a guardian can run a demat account in your name and a mutual fund account for you. The cheapest first step at any age is to learn with virtual money.

Key takeaways

  • From 18, a student can open trading and demat accounts like any adult.
  • Under 18, SEBI's FAQ says a minor can't enter into a contract with a stock broker to buy or sell shares.
  • AMFI's guidelines let a minor be the sole holder of a mutual fund account, with a guardian.
  • Banks may let minors aged 10 or more operate a savings account on their own, on terms each bank sets.
  • AMFI says SIPs can start at ₹500 a month, or ₹250 under Chhoti SIP; StockSaathi's virtual practice is open from age 13.

What can a student do at each age?

Your age decides almost everything. The table sets out the main routes. StockSaathi doesn't recommend any product, so this is a map of what is allowed, not a plan to follow.

Can a student invest in India?

Yes, but how depends on your age. From 18, you can do what any adult does: complete KYC, open a trading and a demat account through a SEBI-registered broker, and place orders. Being a student makes no difference to that, and the student demat guide covers the details.

Under 18, the law treats you as a minor. SEBI's FAQ says a minor can't enter into a contract with a stock broker to buy or sell any security, so you can't trade shares yourself.

A demat account can exist in your name, operated by a guardian, as the guide for teenagers explains. Mutual funds work a little differently: AMFI's guidelines say the minor is the sole holder of the account, there can't be joint holders, and the guardian must be a natural guardian, meaning father or mother, or a court-appointed one. Be wary of anyone who offers a shortcut around these rules. Using someone else's account means the investments legally belong to them, and it often means you have no protection when something goes wrong.

What should you learn before putting in money?

Four things will save you the most pain. What you actually own when you buy a share. How the market and its indices work. How much a price can fall, and how long it can take to come back, which the Nifty recovery guide shows. And how you personally react to a fall, which you can only find out by going through one.

What are the main places money can go?

Broadly: a bank deposit, shares, and mutual funds or ETFs. A deposit is the steadiest. Shares carry the most risk for one company.

Funds and ETFs spread money across many. Which of these suits anyone depends on their situation, which is why StockSaathi explains them without recommending them.

How much is enough to begin?

There is no fixed amount. A single share or a small SIP instalment can be enough, as the guide on how much money you need explains. Only money you won't need soon belongs here, and that applies even more to a student on an allowance.

What mistakes do new investors make?

Buying on tips, which SEBI warns against. Chasing a price that has already jumped. Putting most of their money in one stock. Selling in a panic. These cost nothing to make with virtual money, and the same habits follow you to real money, so it is much better to meet them there first.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 16-year-old invest in the stock market in India?

Not directly through a broker. A guardian can operate a demat account in your name, and a mutual fund account can be held with a guardian, but you can't contract with a broker until 18.

How can a college student start investing?

If you are 18 or over, you complete KYC and open bank, trading and demat accounts. Learning with virtual money first costs nothing.

Is it worth investing as a student with little money?
