Mutual fund costs explained: expense ratio and exit load

By Ali Arbab, founder of StockSaathi · Updated 7 November 2026

An expense ratio is the yearly cost of running a fund, taken out of its assets, and an exit load is a fee for leaving within a set period. Neither arrives as a bill, so they are easy to overlook. Over decades even a small yearly cost can take a large share of your final amount.

Key takeaways

  • An expense ratio is a yearly cost taken from the fund's assets, so it shows up as a lower NAV.
  • SEBI's 2026 Regulations cap the base expense ratio at 0.90% for index funds and ETFs, and at 2.10% on the first slab of an active equity fund.
  • An exit load is charged when you redeem units, and it can't exceed 3% of NAV.
  • ₹1,00,000 at an assumed 10% a year grows to ₹6,72,750 in 20 years with no cost, but to ₹5,60,441 with a cost of 1% a year.

How much can costs add up to over time?

Far more than the yearly figure suggests, because a cost taken each year also removes the growth that money would have earned. The table takes ₹1,00,000, an assumed return of 10% a year before costs and no further money added. These rates are invented to show the arithmetic, and no fund is promised any return.

What is an expense ratio?

An expense ratio is the yearly cost of running a fund, shown as a percentage of its assets. It covers things like managing the money, administration and distribution. You never pay it directly: it is taken from the fund's assets, a little at a time, so it shows up as a slightly lower NAV. That is why people forget about it. A fund's reported returns already have it deducted.

What are SEBI's current limits?

The rules changed recently, so older articles may quote different figures. Under the SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 2026, in force from 1 April 2026, the limit is a base expense ratio. For an index fund or ETF it is 0.90%. For an open-ended equity-oriented scheme it starts at 2.10% on the first ₹500 crore of daily net assets and falls in steps as the fund grows, down to 0.95% on the largest amounts. Other open-ended schemes start lower, at 1.85%.

The total you bear can be higher than the base figure, because the Regulations add brokerage cost, transaction cost and statutory levies on top. They also say no other charges can be made to investors beyond these and the exit load. These are upper limits, and many funds charge less. A fund's own documents give its actual figure, which can change, so check it there.

The limits also show why size matters: as a fund grows, the cap falls, which spreads the costs of running it across more money. Index funds have the lowest cap of all, since nobody is being paid to pick shares. When you compare two funds, compare what they actually charge in the same plan type, because a Direct plan and a Regular plan of the same scheme will differ.

What is an exit load?

An exit load is a charge or fee levied at the time of redemption, when you sell your units back. Funds typically use it to discourage people from leaving early, so it often applies only within a set period. Under SEBI's Regulations, an open-ended scheme's exit load can't exceed 3% of NAV.

How do direct and regular plans differ in cost?

AMFI's booklet explains that both belong to the same scheme, with the same portfolio and the same manager. Regular plans involve distribution expenses or commission, and direct plans don't, so direct plans generally have a lower expense ratio. SEBI's FAQ says mutual funds have had to offer a direct plan since 1 January 2013. A regular plan pays for someone's service, so the question is whether you need that service.

Are low costs the only thing that matters?

No. Costs are one of the few things you can know in advance, which is why they get attention, but they aren't the only thing. A cheap fund that doesn't suit you is still the wrong fund. The guide to index and active funds compares two very different cost levels, and the SIP guide shows how returns build up over time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good expense ratio?

There is no single good number, since it depends on the type of fund. Index funds usually cost less than active funds. Compare similar funds, in the same type of plan.

Is the expense ratio deducted from my money?

Yes, but indirectly. It is taken from the fund's assets and shows up as a lower NAV, so you never see a separate charge.

How much is an exit load?

It varies by fund, and many apply it only within a set period. SEBI's Regulations cap it at 3% of NAV for open-ended schemes.

Expense ratio kya hota hai?

Expense ratio fund chalane ka saalana kharcha hai jo fund ke assets se kat jaata hai, isliye NAV thoda kam dikhta hai. Exit load jaldi nikalne par lagne wali fees hai.
