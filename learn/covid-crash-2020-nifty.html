What happened to ₹1 lakh in the Nifty 50 during the COVID crash (2020)

By Ali Arbab, founder of StockSaathi · Updated 2 November 2026

₹1,00,000 put into the Nifty 50 on 19 February 2020 was worth ₹62,760 by 23 March, after a 37.2% fall in 33 days. Someone who held on had ₹1,02,764 by 9 November 2020. Someone who panic-sold on day three kept ₹97,295 in cash and missed the climb back.

Key takeaways

  • ₹1,00,000 tracking the Nifty 50 from 19 February 2020 fell to ₹62,760 at the lowest close on 23 March 2020, a 37.2% drop in 33 days.
  • On that last day of the fall the Nifty dropped 13.0% in one session, the day before India's 21-day national lockdown was announced.
  • Holding all the way left ₹1,02,764 on 9 November 2020, 2.8% more than the start.
  • Selling everything on day three left ₹97,295, 2.7% less than the start, so holding finished 5.6% ahead.
  • The Nifty was back at its 19 February level on 6 November 2020, 157 trading days after the bottom.

What happened to the Nifty 50 during the COVID crash?

The Nifty 50 fell 37.2% from 19 February to 23 March 2020 and then spent the rest of the year climbing back. The table follows ₹1,00,000 that moves exactly with the index, next to the same money sold on day three and kept in cash.

How much did ₹1 lakh lose at the bottom of the COVID crash?

At the lowest close, ₹1,00,000 had become ₹62,760: a loss of ₹37,240 on paper in 33 days. The worst single day was the last one. On 23 March 2020 the Nifty fell 13.0% and closed at 7,610.25. The next evening, the Prime Minister announced a complete national lockdown for 21 days, and on 11 March the World Health Organization had already said COVID-19 could be characterised as a pandemic.

Nobody knew on 23 March that it was the bottom. That is the part a chart drawn afterwards hides. The news was getting worse, not better, and the index had fallen almost every week for a month. From the inside, there was no signal that the fall had ended.

What if you had sold on day three?

Selling on day three felt sensible at the time. You would have ended with ₹97,295, 2.7% less than the start, while holding reached ₹1,02,764 by 9 November 2020. On 25 February the Nifty was only 2.7% below the start, the virus was spreading outside China, and getting out with ₹97,295 looked like dodging a disaster. For a month it did: while the held portfolio sank to ₹62,760, the seller's cash stayed at ₹97,295.

Then the market turned without warning. By November the held portfolio was back above the seller's cash, and on 9 November 2020 it was worth ₹1,02,764 against ₹97,295. Holding finished 5.6% ahead. The seller avoided the pain of the fall but also missed the whole climb back, which is the usual cost of a panic sale: selling because the price is falling fast, not because anything about the investment has changed.

When did the Nifty recover from the COVID crash?

The Nifty 50 closed back at its 19 February level, 12,125.90, on 6 November 2020, 157 trading days after the bottom. Three days later, on 9 November, it closed above its January 2020 high of 12,362.30. A trading day is a day the exchange is open, roughly 250 a year, so the recovery from the bottom took about seven and a half months. That was quick by the index's own history: the record of every 10% Nifty fall since 2010 shows a typical 10% fall took about nine and a half months to undo, even though COVID-19 was by far the deepest of them.

Does holding always beat selling in a crash?

No, and it matters to be honest about that. In the COVID-19 crash holding won because the market recovered within the same year. In the 2008 crisis the Nifty fell much further and took far longer to come back, so someone who sold early in 2008 was still well ahead ten months later; you can compare the two in the 2008 crash replay.

The useful takeaway from 2020 is narrower: a fast, frightening fall is not proof that it will keep falling, and decisions made in the worst week tend to be driven by fear rather than by anything that changed at the companies themselves. Nothing here says what the next crash will do.

What would a ₹5,000 monthly SIP have done through the crash?

It would have been down sharply at the bottom and up by November. A SIP, or systematic investment plan, puts a fixed amount in at regular intervals. Put ₹5,000 into the Nifty 50 on its January 2020 high and again on the first session of each month, and ₹15,000 had gone in by 23 March 2020, worth ₹9,746, a fall of 35.0%.

By 9 November 2020, ₹55,000 had gone in and it was worth ₹64,474, a gain of 17.2%, because the instalments in the following months bought at low prices. This uses the index itself, so it leaves out fund costs and dividends. It shows how regular buying works out in one fall, not what will happen in the next.

How did the COVID crash compare with the 2008 fall?

COVID was faster and shallower. The Nifty 50 fell 38.4% in 47 trading sessions and was back at its old high 205 sessions after the peak. In 2008 it fell 59.9% over 198 sessions and took 694 sessions to get back. Both are called crashes, but one felt like a sudden shock and the other like a long grind, and the 2008 guide shows why early sellers came out ahead that time.

When did the Indian stock market recover after the COVID crash?

Is it a mistake to sell when the market crashes?

Not always. Selling because something about the investment has changed is different from selling only because the price is falling. In the 2020 crash, selling early locked in a loss that holding later recovered; in 2008, selling early turned out better within the year.
