The disposition effect: selling winners too early

The disposition effect is the tendency to sell investments that have gone up too soon, and to hold those that have gone down for too long. Selling a winner feels good and selling a loser feels like admitting a mistake, so the portfolio slowly fills with losers. Terrance Odean documented the pattern in 1998.

What is the disposition effect?

The term comes from behavioural finance. In his paper "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?", published in the Journal of Finance, Terrance Odean describes it as the tendency of investors to hold losing investments too long and sell winning investments too soon. He tested it on the trading records of 10,000 brokerage accounts at a large discount brokerage house from 1987 to 1993.

The investors showed a strong preference for realising winners rather than losers. He also reported that this behaviour wasn't justified by how the investments performed later, and that for taxable investments it led to lower after-tax returns.

Those were American investors decades ago, not Indian students, so the numbers don't transfer directly. But the feeling behind it is common to everyone. Selling at a profit gives you a small, certain win. Selling at a loss turns a number on a screen into a real mistake with your name on it, and most people will go a long way to put that moment off.

Why do we hold losers and sell winners?

Because of what each sale means. A paper loss still feels temporary, so holding keeps the hope alive. A sale makes it final.

That is the same trap as anchoring on the price you paid, where your purchase price becomes a level the share owes you. But your purchase price means nothing to the market, and a share doesn't know what you paid.

How is this different from panic selling?

They run in opposite directions. Panic selling is getting out of a falling share too quickly out of fear. The disposition effect is often about refusing to sell a falling share at all, and getting out of a rising one too early.

The same person can do both at different times. What links them is that the decision came from a feeling about a price, rather than from anything about the business.

How can you check your own trades?

Go through your past sales and mark each as a gain or a loss. Then list what you still hold and mark those too. If the sales are nearly all gains and the holdings are mostly losses, the pattern may be yours.

What habits reduce it?

Ask the same question of every holding: "If I had the cash instead, would I buy this today, at this price?" That question ignores what you paid, which is the point. Write down when you buy what would make you sell, and base it on the business. And look at your whole portfolio together rather than each share's gain or loss, so that no single number decides for you.

Frequently asked questions

What is an example of the disposition effect?

Selling a share that is up 18% to lock in the gain, while keeping one that is down 22% until it comes back to what you paid.

Is selling a winner a bad thing?

Not on its own. There are good reasons to sell. The disposition effect is about a pattern, where gains are sold quickly and losses are never sold, for emotional reasons.

Why is it called the disposition effect?

It refers to investors' disposition, or tendency, to sell winners and keep losers. Researchers in behavioural finance use the term for this pattern.
