Demonetisation and the stock market: what happened in 2016

After demonetisation was announced on 8 November 2016, the Nifty 50 slid for seven weeks, losing 7.4% by its low on 26 December 2016. It was back above its starting level by 25 January 2017. It was a real fall but a shallow one, far smaller than the 2008 or COVID-19 crashes.

Key takeaways

  • ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes stopped being legal tender at midnight on 8 November 2016, after the Prime Minister's address that evening.
  • The Nifty 50 fell 2.9% in the first three sessions and 7.4% by its low on 26 December 2016.
  • ₹1,00,000 in the index at the 8 November close was worth ₹92,564 at the low.
  • By 28 February 2017 the index stood 3.9% above where it started.
  • The RBI later reported that about 99.3% of the value of the withdrawn notes came back.

What was demonetisation?

Demonetisation means withdrawing a currency note's status as legal money. On the evening of 8 November 2016 the Prime Minister announced that ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes would no longer be legal tender from midnight. The RBI's notice the same day gave the aims as tackling counterfeit notes, nullifying black money hoarded in cash and curbing the funding of terrorism with fake notes.

From 10 November people could deposit old notes in bank accounts or exchange a limited amount over the counter, and deposits ran until 30 December 2016. ATMs stayed shut on 9 November, and cash withdrawals from bank accounts were capped at first. For a few weeks, a country that used a great deal of cash had to find new ways to pay, and notes of ₹100 and below, along with all coins, stayed in use.

Why did the Nifty fall after the note ban?

Investors were pricing in uncertainty. If people can't get cash, shops sell less, and companies that depend on cash customers may earn less for a while. Nobody knew how long the shortage would last or how deep it would go, so prices fell while that question stayed open. The market was also reacting to other news in those weeks, so the whole fall can't be pinned on one cause.

That is a useful thing to notice as a beginner. Headlines give a single reason for a price move because a single reason makes a clean story. Real prices are the combined result of many people acting on many things at once.

How long did the market take to recover?

Not long. The Nifty 50 hit its low of 7,908.25 on 26 December 2016, 48 calendar days after the announcement, and closed back above 8,543.55 on 25 January 2017, only 22 trading days after the low. By 28 February it stood at 8,879.60. A year after the low, on 26 December 2017, it closed at 10,531.50.

This fall never reached 10%, so it doesn't appear in the record of 10% falls in the Nifty 50. Someone who sold on the third day ended February with ₹97,106, while someone who held had ₹1,03,933, a gap of 7.0 percentage points. That result isn't a rule, though: in the 2008 crash, selling early came out ahead.

Did most of the money come back to the banks?

Yes. The RBI's Annual Report for 2017-18 put the withdrawn notes at ₹15,417.93 billion and the notes returned at ₹15,310.73 billion, which works out to about 99.3% by value. That figure arrived almost two years after the announcement, long after the market had moved on. Prices react to what people expect at the time, not to what the final numbers later show.

What can beginners learn from it?

The main thing is how a slow fall feels. A crash makes the news, but a fall of a few tenths of a percent most days for seven weeks wears you down in a quieter way, and panic selling doesn't need a dramatic day to happen. Having a written reason for what you hold helps you tell a policy shock from something that has changed about the investment itself.

Frequently asked questions

How much did the stock market fall after demonetisation?

The Nifty 50 fell 7.4% from its close of 8,543.55 on 8 November 2016 to 7,908.25 on 26 December 2016, measured on daily closes.

When did the stock market recover after demonetisation?

The Nifty 50 closed back above its 8 November level on 25 January 2017, 22 trading days after its low.

Was demonetisation a stock market crash?

Not by the usual measure. The fall stayed under 10%, far smaller than the 38.4% fall in the COVID-19 crash or the 59.9% fall in 2008.
