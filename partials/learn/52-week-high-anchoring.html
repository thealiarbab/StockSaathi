What is a 52-week high? And the anchoring trap for beginners

A 52-week high is the highest price a share reached in the past year, and a 52-week low is its lowest. They are useful markers, but they say nothing about what a share is worth. The trap is anchoring: treating an old price as the price the share should return to.

What is the 52-week high and low?

The 52-week high is the highest price in the past year and the 52-week low is the lowest. Apps usually show them with the current price, so you can see at a glance where a share sits in its range. The window rolls forward every day, so an old high simply drops out after a year. Different sites may use closing prices or prices during the day, and may or may not adjust for splits, which is why the numbers don't always match.

What is anchoring bias in investing?

Anchoring is letting a first number shape your later estimate, even when it shouldn't. In their 1974 paper in Science, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that people make estimates by starting from an initial value and adjusting, and that the adjustment is typically too small. Different starting points lead to different answers.

Their experiments had nothing to do with shares, so applying the idea to prices is our inference, but the fit is easy to see. If a share once traded at ₹1,000 and now trades at ₹700, ₹1,000 tends to stick in your mind as the "real" price, and ₹700 starts to feel like a discount.

The ₹1,000 tells you what somebody paid at one moment, under the facts known at that moment. It says nothing about what the business is worth now. The same thing happens with the price you paid yourself, which often becomes the number you are waiting to get back to, as though the share knows or cares what you paid for it. That second anchor is the more dangerous one, because it is the one that ties up your money and your attention.

Is a stock near its 52-week low a bargain?

Not because of that alone. A low price can reflect real bad news. We looked at every new 250-session low and high in today's large Indian stocks since 2012, to see what happened a year later.

The medians are close, at +17.0% after a new low and +15.15% after a new high, so position in the range told you little. What differs is the worst tenth: −25.96% against −19.15%. And this list only contains companies that survived, so the true record after a new low is worse than it looks. A stock near its high isn't automatically expensive, and one near its low isn't automatically cheap.

How can you avoid anchoring?

Ask a different question. Instead of "how far is this from its high?", ask "would I buy this today at this price if I had never seen the old one?" Look at what the business earns, using measures such as the P/E ratio, rather than where the chart has been. And notice the opposite pull: a price that has just shot up isn't proof of value either, which is the FOMO trap.
