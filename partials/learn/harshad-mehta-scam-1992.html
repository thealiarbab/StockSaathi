The Harshad Mehta scam explained for students

The 1992 securities scam, linked to the broker Harshad Mehta, involved irregular deals in government securities and bank money diverted to brokers' accounts. The Government set up a Special Court by ordinance on 6 June 1992, and the RBI banned key inter-bank deals that month. It changed how Indian markets are policed.

Key takeaways

  • Official records describe large-scale irregularities in government and other securities transactions, and diversion of funds from banks to the accounts of certain brokers.
  • The RBI records banks doing ready forward deals without holding the securities, and using bank receipts instead.
  • A Special Court ordinance was promulgated on 6 June 1992, and 29 entities were notified under the law that replaced it.
  • The RBI banned inter-bank ready forward deals in dated government securities from 22 June 1992.
  • The SEBI Act, 1992 is deemed to have come into force on 30 January 1992, the same year.

What does the official record show?

This guide sticks to dated facts from primary documents. You will find larger claims elsewhere, including amounts of money and index levels, which we couldn't confirm from an official source, so they aren't repeated here.

What was the 1992 securities scam?

The law passed in response describes it plainly. Its Statement of Objects and Reasons, as quoted by the Supreme Court, says large scale irregularities and malpractices were noticed in transactions in both government and other securities. Those findings came out of RBI investigations, and it speaks of diversion of funds from banks and financial institutions to the individual accounts of certain brokers.

Harshad Shantilal Mehta was the best-known broker involved, and his name appears on the court cases that followed. A person's guilt on any particular charge is for the courts, and this guide doesn't go beyond what those records say.

How did the scheme work?

It relied on gaps in how banks traded government securities with one another. In a ready forward deal, one bank sells securities to another and agrees to buy them back. The RBI's later summary records banks doing these deals without holding enough securities, in physical form or in their official ledger account.

Instead of the securities, they passed around bank receipts, paper that stood in for securities the bank didn't really hold. The RBI also notes that banks used such deals to dress up their profitability or their required holdings.

The essential weakness was simple: one party was relying on a piece of paper as if it were the asset. When paper can stand in for a security with nobody checking, money can move on the strength of a promise. That is exactly what proper settlement exists to stop.

It is why today's electronic records and a fixed settlement cycle matter so much, even if they seem dull. For a student, that is the core idea to take away from the whole episode.

What changed afterwards?

Quite a lot, and quickly. An ordinance on 6 June 1992 set up a Special Court and a Custodian to deal with these transactions, and it was later replaced by an Act. The Supreme Court records that 29 individuals and companies were notified under it and that their holdings were attached. The RBI, acting on the initial report of the Janakiraman Committee, banned inter-bank ready forward deals in dated government securities from 22 June 1992, and told banks to keep records of receipts issued and received.

The same year gave India a regulator with legal teeth. The SEBI Act, 1992 set up SEBI to protect the interests of investors and to develop and regulate the securities market. The Act was already in preparation before the scam became public knowledge, so it shouldn't be read as simply a response to it, but together they mark the start of the modern system.

What can a beginner learn from it?

Two things stand out. First, ask what you actually own. A record that says you hold something isn't the same as holding it, which is why a demat account with a regulated depository matters.

Second, a market can rise for reasons that have nothing to do with how companies are doing. Rules can reduce that risk, but not remove it, and a rising price on its own isn't proof of anything.

Frequently asked questions

What did Harshad Mehta do?

He was the best-known broker linked to the 1992 securities scam. Official records describe irregular securities transactions and diversion of bank funds to the accounts of certain brokers.

What is a bank receipt in the 1992 scam?

A paper issued by a bank standing in for government securities. The RBI records banks using them in ready forward deals without holding enough actual securities.

Did the scam create SEBI?

Not exactly. The SEBI Act is deemed in force from 30 January 1992, and SEBI was established on 12 April 1992. The scam showed why strong regulation mattered.

Harshad Mehta scam kya tha?

1992 mein government securities ke saudon mein badi gadbad pakdi gayi, aur banks ka paisa kuch brokers ke accounts mein gaya. Iske baad 6 June 1992 ko Special Court ka ordinance aaya.
