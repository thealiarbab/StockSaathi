What should come before investing your first salary?

SEBI's financial education booklet treats investing as one step in a plan. You begin by working out where you stand. In practice, that means knowing what comes in each month, what goes out, and what you owe.

SEBI's material lists situations that money has to cover, including emergencies such as illness or an accident. So people usually think about three things before investing: keeping cash they can reach quickly, dealing with expensive debt, and being clear about what the money is for.

Debt matters because a loan charging a high rate is hard for any investment to beat. Money you will need within a year or two is also poorly suited to shares, because markets can fall and stay down, as the Nifty recovery guide shows. How much to set aside depends on your own life, and StockSaathi doesn't give a number.

Your first few months of earning also tell you what your real costs are, which is useful to learn before you commit anything. A rough plan that you actually follow is worth more than a clever one you abandon.

What are the main places money can go?

Broadly, bank deposits, shares, and mutual funds or ETFs. A deposit is the steadiest and earns a stated interest rate. A share is a slice of one company, with the most risk from that one business.

A fund spreads money across many holdings, and the guide to funds, stocks and ETFs compares them. Each has different risks and costs, and the right mix depends on you. Under India's tax rules, some products have special treatment: the ELSS guide covers one of them.

How do people invest a part of each month's pay?

Many use a SIP, which puts a fixed amount into a mutual fund at regular intervals. AMFI says the instalment could be as small as ₹500 a month. Its main benefit is that it turns a decision you would have to keep making into a habit, and it doesn't remove the risk of the fund falling.

What paperwork is needed to start?

KYC documents showing your identity and address, which SEBI's booklet says are mandatory, and a bank account. For shares you also need a trading account and a demat account, which the step-by-step guide explains.

What should you do with a pay rise or a bonus?

The same thinking applies. People often raise their spending as fast as their income, so a useful habit is to decide in advance what share of any increase goes where. A one-off bonus can also go towards goals you set earlier, such as clearing a costly loan or building your emergency money, before you think about investing it.

How can you avoid the common traps?

Ignore anyone promising fixed or guaranteed returns from shares. SEBI says not to rely on hot tips, and it warns against unsolicited advice from unregistered advisers. If you want advice about your own situation, SEBI's booklet says investors may approach a SEBI-registered investment adviser, and you can check registration on SEBI's website. Frequently asked questions

How much of my first salary should I invest?

StockSaathi doesn't give a number, because it depends on your income, costs and debts. A common first step is knowing what you spend and setting money aside for emergencies.

Should I start a SIP with my first salary?

That is your decision. A SIP is a way of investing a fixed amount regularly, and it suits money you won't need soon, but it doesn't remove the risk of loss.

Do I need a demat account to invest?

For shares, yes. Mutual funds can be bought without one. SEBI's booklet lists bank, trading and demat accounts as what you need for shares.
