Nifty 500 stocks still below their last peak

Most Indian stocks sit below their old highs at any given time, and some have stayed there for many years. At the end of October 2026, 89.6% of 423 stocks in the Nifty 500 were at least 10% below their highest close since 2011, and 23.2% were at least half below it.

Key takeaways

  • Of 423 stocks studied, 89.6% were 10% or more below their highest close since 2011 at the end of October 2026.
  • 53.2% were 30% or more below it, and 23.2% were 50% or more below it.
  • The median stock was 32.7% below its high, while the Nifty 50 was 12.4% below its own.
  • Some highs date from 2011, so a share can stay below a peak for more than a decade.

What is a drawdown?

A drawdown is how far a price has fallen from its previous high. If a share peaked at ₹100 and trades at ₹60, its drawdown is 40%. The arithmetic is harsher on the way back: a 40% fall needs a rise of about 67% to return to the old level, and a 90% fall needs a rise of 900%. That is why deep drawdowns are so hard to recover from.

Why can an index hide weak stocks?

Because an index changes its members and weights its biggest companies most. The Nifty 50 was only 12.4% below its high when the median stock in this group was 32.7% below its own. There are three reasons.

A stock that shrinks loses weight in the index and can be replaced, so a long decline stops counting. Stocks reach their highs at different times, so the index never needs them all at their peak together.

And an index spreads the damage across many companies, so one collapse barely moves it. This is the reason the record of the Nifty 50 recovering from every completed 10% fall doesn't carry over to a single share. The index has a way of leaving its losers behind, and a shareholder holding one of those losers has no such option. It is one of the plainest arguments for looking at what you own rather than at what the market did.

Do all stocks recover after a fall?

No. Many falls do recover: the large-cap recovery data shows how quickly some companies climbed back. But being below a high isn't a temporary state that fixes itself, and a company's old price isn't a level it owes you. This list also understates the problem, because companies that went bust or were delisted aren't in it at all.

What does this mean for a beginner?

Don't assume a fallen share will return to its old price. Read the anchoring guide for why that assumption is so tempting, and keep any single holding small enough that a decade below its peak wouldn't sink you. SEBI's investor booklet names this sort of danger plainly: risk that comes from a particular company, as well as risk from the market as a whole.
